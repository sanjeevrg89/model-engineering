GLM-5.3 — 753B, the top open flagship on the 2026 scoreboard — reads one quarter of its cache per step. Latent attention compresses what gets stored; sparse attention cuts what gets read. DeepSeek-V3.2-Exp proved it first: a lightweight indexer scores every past token and full MLA runs over only the top 2,048. This chapter attends over 8,192 past tokens twice: dense MLA on channel A, indexed top-k on channel B.

The 2-minute version

A cheap indexer scores all past tokens; full attention runs over the top 2,048 only. Reads shrink to one quarter while storage stays whole — selection is per query, every step. V3.2-Exp proved it, GLM-5.3 ships it at 753B.

After this chapter you can: explain why DSA saves traffic not capacity · size an indexer against the attention it gates · distinguish top-k from windows.

9.1Score everything, attend to thousands

First time here?

Chapter 0 defines token, vector, head and the attention lookup in ten minutes, with worked numbers from the same two configs. This chapter assumes them.

The indexer is cheap on purpose: small dot-product heads that rank positions without computing values. Only the winners pay for full MLA. With 8,192 past tokens and a top-2,048 selection, the attention kernel reads one quarter of the cache — while storage keeps all 8,192, because next step's winners differ. Selection is per query, every step.

9.2The indexer in the config

V3.2-Exp states the selection in two keys: keep 2,048 positions , renormalize their probabilities . GLM-5.3 keeps the 2,048 and sizes the scorer: 32 index heads of width 128 :

The scorer reads the same compressed representations MLA stores, so indexing adds a small scoring pass instead of a second cache. Thirty-two heads of width 128 against 64 MLA heads of width 256: the indexer is roughly an order of magnitude cheaper than the attention it gates. That ratio is the design constraint — a selector that costs as much as attention would buy nothing.

The concurrent sibling is Native Sparse Attention (DeepSeek, February 2025): three paths per query — compressed coarse tokens, selected fine blocks, sliding window — trained end to end with Triton kernels, matching full attention in the paper's 27B runs. DSA shares the select-over-compressed idea with an inference-time focus; NSA adds trainability and hardware-aligned kernels. The zoo tables the family.

9.3Storage stays dense

The subtlety the figure hides in plain sight: step 2 stores everything on both channels. DSA never deletes; it skips. The indexer's keys must be able to score any past token, and the selected set changes every step, so all latents stay resident. What shrinks is HBM traffic per forward pass — the memory-bound decode's actual bottleneck — not capacity. A 1M-context GLM-5.3 still houses ~90 GB of latent per sequence in bf16; it just doesn't re-read all of it every step. Chapter 23 multiplies this out; chapter 20 budgets the indexer keys beside it.

Easy to get wrong

Top-k is not a window. A window keeps the last 2,048 tokens; the indexer keeps the most relevant 2,048 anywhere in the past — including position 12 in a 100,000-token context. Recency is one signal the scorer can learn, not the mechanism.

9.7Check yourself

With 8,192 past tokens and index_topk 2,048, what fraction of the cache does full attention read?

  1. All of it — top-k only reorders
  2. One quarter: 2,048 winners get MLA, the rest are skipped this step
  3. One eighth: the indexer also halves it
  4. Nothing: the indexer replaces attention
2,048 / 8,192 = 0.25 of reads. Storage stays whole; the winners change next step. The indexer scores but never attends.

From chapter 8: V3.2-Exp's latent cache is 70,272 B per token. What two numbers multiply to it?

  1. 61 layers × 1,024 GQA numbers
  2. 61 layers × 576 latent numbers × 2 B
  3. 2,048 winners × 32 index heads
  4. 256 experts × 274 B each
(512 + 64) × 2 B × 61 layers. The indexer decides what to read; the latent decides what reading costs.

Why must all latents stay resident if only 2,048 are read?

  1. The config forbids eviction
  2. The indexer needs full attention over the past
  3. Next step's winners differ, and any past token must be scoreable and readable
  4. Eviction would break rotary embeddings
Selection is per query per step. A token skipped now can win later, so DSA saves traffic, not capacity — the key distinction from windows.

What does norm_topk_prob do?

  1. Renormalizes the kept probabilities so the winners sum to 1
  2. Normalizes the indexer keys to unit length
  3. Caps the top-k at the window size
  4. Selects the top-k groups instead of tokens
Same role as the router's renormalization in chapter 11: dropped mass must not shrink the output. Both V3.2-Exp and GLM-5.3 set it true.

How does top-k selection differ from a 2,048-token sliding window?

  1. It doesn't — both read 2,048 tokens
  2. The window is learned; top-k is fixed
  3. Top-k reads more: 2,048 per head
  4. Top-k keeps the most relevant 2,048 anywhere in the past; the window keeps the most recent
Relevance versus recency. Position 12 can win top-k in a 100,000-token context; no window reaches it. That is the retrieval case sparse attention exists for.
Takeaways
  • A cheap indexer scores everything; full MLA runs over top-2,048. Reads shrink to one quarter in the figure; storage doesn't.
  • Selection is per query per step — winners change, so all latents stay resident.
  • V3.2-Exp proved it; GLM-5.3 productized it with a sized 32×128 scorer.
  • Kept probabilities renormalize, like router weights. Dropping mass must not shrink outputs.
