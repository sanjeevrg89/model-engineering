On-ramp

Start here

The five ideas every chapter of this course assumes: tokens become vectors, one matrix multiplication, attention as a lookup, normalization, and the residual add. Ten minutes, one square-root symbol. Then chapter 1 reads at config speed.

1Words in, numbers out

A model has never seen a word. It sees tokens: the fragments a tokenizer cuts text into — a common word whole, a rare word in pieces, punctuation alone — and each fragment stores as one integer id. The vocabulary is just the list of possible ids: 32,000 of them in Mistral-7B and 152,064 in Qwen2.5-7B .

The id's job is to pick a row from the model's first table. That row is a list of hidden_size numbers — 3,584 in Qwen2.5-7B , 4,096 in Mistral-7B — and it is the token's vector: the only representation the model ever works with. Dialogue, code and reasoning all enter as one vector per token, position by position.

2The only arithmetic that matters

Everything a block computes reduces to one operation. Take the token's vector — n numbers — and a learned matrix of n rows by m columns, the weights. One output number = one row of the matrix times the whole vector: multiply each pair, add the products. Do that for all m rows and you have m new numbers. The output is called a projection, and it is the whole game.

Keep two things in view. First, nothing here is fancier than multiply-and-add; a 500-billion-parameter checkpoint is a giant catalogue of such grids. Second, the widths tell you the cost: a projection from 3,584 numbers out to 18,944 owns 3,584 × 18,944 weights, and the feed-forward that owns it is the single biggest ingredient of a block — the pattern chapter 1 measures and chapter 22 stores more cheaply.

3Attention is a lookup

Each token's vector needs context from earlier tokens, and attention gathers it in three steps. Every position projects its vector into a query (what am I looking for), while earlier positions project into keys (what each offers) and values (what each hands over when chosen). Matching each query against every key by dot product gives scores; the scores squeeze to positive weights that sum to one; then attention returns the weighted blend of the values. Richly matched tokens donate more, weakly matched ones almost nothing.

The lookup runs in parallel copies called heads — 28 of them in Qwen2.5-7B — each with its own query, key and value matrices, so one head can track syntax while another tracks who "it" refers to. Heads are the machine's eyes; how many get cached, shared or compressed is chapters 1, 6, 8 and 9.

4Normalization

Twenty-eight blocks in a row would drift: vectors could grow, shrink or lean, and nobody downstream asked for it. RMSNorm fixes volume. It divides the incoming vector by its root mean square — the square root of the average of the squared entries — and then scales each entry by a learned gain. The epsilon you will meet in chapter 1, 1e-06 in Qwen2.5-7B , only keeps the division from dividing by zero. After the norm, every block begins with the token at a comparable size, whatever its adventure so far.

5The residual: blocks edit, never replace

Each sub-block in chapter 1 computes its projection output and then adds the original input back in: output = input + change. That is the residual, and its job is depth. The unchanged input always has a clean path around every projection, so the model only ever has to learn the small correction it wants applied, and a 92-layer stack stays trainable where a 92-layer chain of replacements would not.

6That is the whole floor

Five ideas: token to vector, the multiply-and-add projection, the query-key-value lookup, the volume norm, the residual add. Chapter 1 now reads as it was written: two real configs side by side, one block, and the shapes that make 7B a 7B. The glossary keeps definitions handy, and chapter 1 is where the course starts.
